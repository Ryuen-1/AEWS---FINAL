1. INTRODUCTION
   1. Background of the Study
The issue of student dropout has been long leaving a shadow over both personal ambitions and institutional objectives in the Philippines' modern higher education system, especially in state universities and colleges (SUCs). Think about this: the dropout rate in higher education is a startling 39% nationwide, with almost 4 out of 10 students in SUCs leaving their programs before finishing them. This number is made worse by factors including lack of interest in school and concerns regarding employability (Bravo, 2023). For universities and colleges, this rate translates to about 35.15% dropout estimated for the 2023–2024 academic year; in areas like Northern Mindanao and Camarines Sur, where socioeconomic vulnerabilities worsen the problem, the numbers are significantly higher (PHKULE, 2025; Villanueva, 2024).
As the country strives for inclusive growth, these dropout rates rise even more for underrepresented groups, such as low-income or first-generation students at SUCs, extending cycles of inequality in a time when a college degree is still an essential entry point to economic mobility. This educational inequality is not confined only within national borders because student retention has a direct impact on the global economy. In today's interconnected knowledge economy, the inability of higher education institutions to retain and produce skilled graduates limits not only national competitiveness, but also the global talent pipeline that supports innovation and sustainable industry growth (World Bank, 2024).
Addressing student retention is in line with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 4 (Quality Education), which aims to provide inclusive and equitable education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all (United Nations, 2023). While society has lower productivity and a higher dependency on social services, SUCs are faced with income losses from empty seats and difficulties in recruitment. According to recent studies by the Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM 2), high dropout rates leads to billions of money in lost investments in free higher education under Republic Act 10931. The main reasons given for dropout are monetary difficulties and post-pandemic disruptions. (EDCOM 2, 2025; PHKULE, 2025).
Enrollment in Philippine higher education has fluctuated between 2020 and 2025, suggesting a "learning crisis," which implies pressures affecting SUCs' contributions to the country's development (PHKULE, 2025). The need has only increased due to the post-pandemic escalation of these patterns, which were characterized by interrupted blended learning and increased vulnerability in rural SUCs. Although there is much proof of these issues, traditional solutions frequently come too late, acting only after grades have fallen and motivation has dropped, leaving students at risk stranded in a system that is not prepared to prevent them. According to recent findings, AI-driven learning analytics have been proven to enhance academic monitoring accuracy by over 85%, using models that correlate engagement metrics and emotional sentiment analysis from online forums (Zawacki-Richter et al., 2023; Ahmed & Haseeb, 2024).
Reliance on reactive academic support systems, which, in spite of their best efforts, usually fail to proactively address underlying problems, is at the core of the retention crisis at Philippine SUCs. Under the Student Affairs and Services (SAS) framework of CHED Memorandum Order No. 09, Series of 2013, traditional methods like manual advising sessions, end-of-semester progress reviews, and independent tutoring operate within clusters, reacting to clear warning signs of failure rather than predicting them (CHED, 2013; Villanueva, 2025). Due to their heavy caseloads, faculty and advisors at SUCs frequently fail to notice subtle signs such as irregular attendance, late submissions, or declining interest in learning management systems (LMS), especially when dealing with numerous groups of students whose needs differ greatly between urban and rural campuses (Sayo et al., 2022).
According to a 2025 study into student support services at a state institution, many students in areas like Camarines Sur felt unsupported throughout the shift to online learning due to uneven access and insufficient personalization (Sayo et al., 2022). According to qualitative reports from education graduate students at SUCs, there is a gap between K–12 and higher education support systems, and reactive interventions fall short in establishing foundational skills or fostering self-efficacy at an early age. This is made worse by issues like a lack of institutional resources (Hara et al., 2023). These problems are made worse by resource limitations: fragmented data systems and underfunded advising centers make it difficult to scale, particularly in light of the anticipated enrollment challenges in 2025 and the changing academic roles in SUCs (Arao et al., 2025).
The current structure not only normalize inequalities but also puts a strain on institutional budgets, shifting funds from innovation to remediation in a setting where 40% of undergraduates drop out overall and freshman dropout hovers at 25% in some SUCs (GMA Network, 2024; ONE News, 2025). Ultimately, reactive systems deal with symptoms rather than causes, pointing out the need for data-driven strategies that intervene before trajectories go off course.
In the Philippine setting, the potential of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) represents an abrupt shift toward personalized, predictive education. According to recent findings, AI-driven learning analytics have been proven to enhance academic monitoring accuracy by over 85%, using models that correlate engagement metrics and emotional sentiment analysis from online forums (Zawacki-Richter et al., 2023; Ahmed & Haseeb, 2024). In line with the Department of Education's drive for digital transformation, artificial intelligence (AI) has been growing in popularity in higher education over the last five years. It uses massive data sets from learning management systems (LMS) like Moodle and Canvas to find patterns that are unseen to the human eye (De la Cruz, 2025). SUCs can now predict outcomes and customize assistance with previously unheard-of precision thanks to predictive analytics in particular (Alotaibi & Alshehri, 2025).
This trend is clearly shown in systematic reviews conducted between 2021 and 2025. With applications tested in Philippine settings, a 2025 analysis of machine learning models for student achievement prediction showed how algorithms can process behavioral, demographic, and performance data to improve retention by identifying at-risk students weeks or months in advance (Baticulon et al., 2025; Maguigad et al., 2025). Technologies in adaptive learning, where generative AI improves engagement and outcomes by 15-20% in individualized paths, were also identified in a thorough literature review on AI in education. Examples of these technologies include SIGLA and TALINO for Philippine public education (Guevara et al., 2025).
These tools effortlessly translate raw data into actionable foresight for SUCs by drawing parallels from adjacent sectors, such as AI-driven predictive maintenance in healthcare, which decreases mistakes by 30%, or fraud detection in banking (Hassan et al., 2025). Predictive analytics, for example, has been demonstrated to increase success rates in STEM subjects by incorporating clickstream data from online courses, promoting early interventions that suit a variety of learning styles in settings with limited resources (Khalil et al., 2025). However, as adoption increases—by 2025, more than 70% of Philippine institutions will be experimenting with AI—ethical issues including algorithmic bias and data privacy will still be important to fair implementation in SUCs (Instructure, 2025). Though there is promise in this technology boom, its full potential in developing comprehensive early warning systems for student performance is still not fully realized in the local context.
However, despite the promising outcomes of prior research, no existing framework has yet operationalized an AI-powered EWS specifically adapted for the localized data realities and infrastructure constraints of Philippine SUCs. Most studies remain theoretical or limited to pilot-scale applications without institutional adoption or integration into the CHED SAS framework, revealing a critical implementation gap this study aims to address. (Liao et al., 2025; Mahmood et al., 2025). A 2025 systematic review of 51 articles highlights key limitations, including overreliance on demographic variables, neglect of psychosocial factors (e.g., mental health proxies), and insufficient focus on underrepresented populations, leading to biased outcomes that exacerbate inequities in diverse Philippine settings (Maguigad et al., 2025). While recent investigations emphasize the need for multi-modal data fusion—integrating LMS logs, attendance records, and sentiment analysis for nuanced, interpretable alerts—few address rural-urban divides in SUCs (Rodriguez et al., 2024); moreover, current models often prioritize accuracy over actionability, failing to generate tailored interventions like personalized tutoring amid local challenges such as connectivity issues (Baticulon et al., 2025). Although predictive tools exist, few bridge to proactive, scalable EWS empowering educators with timely, equitable insights; this study addresses this void by proposing an AI framework that not only predicts but prescribes. 
The idea of an AI-Powered Predictive Analytics platform for an Academic Early Warning System that reimagines support as a dynamic safety net for Philippine SUCs makes this capstone research stand out as an important solution. The system will provide real-time alarms, personalized intervention routes, and continuous dashboards to promote academic resilience by combining multi-source data using sophisticated machine learning classifiers, such as ensemble models that can identify at-risk individuals with up to 92% accuracy (Arao et al., 2025).
Beyond academic performance, emerging research indicates that psychosocial factors significantly influence student retention in Philippine higher education. Studies by Lipson et al. (2019) and Auer & Griffiths (2023) demonstrate that mental health challenges, including anxiety, depression, and social isolation, contribute substantially to student attrition, particularly in post-pandemic educational contexts. In Philippine State Universities and Colleges, where students often face compounded socioeconomic pressures, these non-academic factors can be as critical as academic performance in predicting dropout risk. Recent investigations by Pritchard & Wilson (2023) found that incorporating psychosocial indicators into early warning systems improved retention prediction accuracy by 18-22%, highlighting the limitations of exclusively academic monitoring approaches. This evidence underscores the necessity for holistic early warning systems that address both academic and counseling needs to effectively support student success.
   2. Objectives of the Study
The primary objective of this capstone project is to develop an AI-powered Academic Early Warning System (AEWS) utilizing predictive analytics to identify at-risk students in Philippine SUCs, enabling proactive interventions that enhance student retention and institutional performance. Specifically, this study aims to:
1. Analyze multi-source institutional data from College of Technologies at Bukidnon State University for feature extraction and as inputs to the predictive model.
2. Design a role-based secure web system compliant with data privacy laws and aligned with CHED SAS and AMU access guidelines.
3. Develop a role-based web system that detects and provides early warning analytics using XGBoost prediction model.
4. Test accuracy of the AI model using standard metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC.
5. Evaluate the software’s functionality, usability, and reliability.




   3. Significance of the Study
This study on developing an AI-Powered Academic Early Warning System (AEWS) for student performance prediction holds profound implications for transforming higher education in Philippine State Universities and Colleges (SUCs), particularly through its pilot implementation at Bukidnon State University (BSU) in Region 10, Northern Mindanao.
At-Risk Students. The system aims to provide early, personalized alerts and intervention recommendations to address academic challenges proactively, reducing dropout risks and fostering greater resilience and success in their educational journeys.
Faculty and Academic Advisors. The system equips educators with actionable, data-driven insights into student performance trends, enabling more targeted mentoring and support that alleviates workload burdens and enhances teaching effectiveness.
Guidance Counselors and Student Affairs Personnel: The system will provide early identification of students exhibiting signs of emotional distress, social integration challenges, or other non-academic difficulties, enabling proactive counseling referrals and psychosocial support services. This enhances the capacity of student support units to address the holistic needs of at-risk students beyond purely academic concerns.

Commission on Higher Education (CHED) and Policymakers: This research offers empirical evidence and a replicable framework for integrating AI into national higher education strategies, supporting broader goals of reducing attrition rates and promoting inclusive access under Republic Act 10931.
Future Researchers: This study provides a foundational blueprint, open-source methodologies, and pilot data from Bukidnon State University, inspiring subsequent investigations into AI-driven educational tools, ethical AI applications, and longitudinal impacts on retention in diverse SUC contexts.
   4. Scope and Delimitations.
The present capstone project includes the design, development, and testing of a working Academic Early Warning System (AEWS) for Bukidnon State University. The system focuses on identifying students who may need early academic assistance and on simplifying the Academic Mentoring Unit (AMU) referral procedure. It is implemented as a role-based web application for administrators, instructors, AMU staff, and students.
The AEWS encompasses several primary features designed to support role-based workflows. Functional capabilities include user authentication and account access control, class management, student enrollment management, grade and attendance tracking, AI-assisted risk prediction using an XGBoost classification model, automatic and manual referral generation, digital needs-assessment form handling, intervention and support routing, in-system and email notifications, activity logging, search and filtering, archive management, and role-specific dashboards and reporting.
The pilot study focuses on Bukidnon State University. The data used by the implemented system consists of institutional and user-managed academic records available through the AEWS workflow, including class records, student enrollment data, gradesheets, attendance records, referral information, and needs-assessment responses. Although the broader conceptual discussion recognizes possible inputs such as LMS engagement data, demographic indicators, behavioral logs, and sentiment-based indicators, these are not fully integrated as live data sources in the current implementation.
The system includes a limited student-facing module that allows students to access their account, view relevant academic and referral-related information, receive notifications, and complete assigned needs-assessment forms. However, it does not include a mobile application, chatbot-based counseling, live counseling sessions, or fully automated intervention tracking. The system is intended to support academic early warning, AMU referral processing, support-routing documentation, and operational reporting rather than function as a complete institutional data integration platform.
The study is restricted to the functionality, usability, and operational reliability of the implemented web system and its facilitation of the existing academic monitoring and referral workflow. Extensive retention outcome measurement, cross-institutional implementation, live LMS or SIS integration, and long-term automated intervention monitoring are outside the scope of this study.
2. REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE
   1. Related Literature
      1. The State of Student Dropout and Retention in Higher Education
Student attrition has been a nagging issue for the individual academic goal and institutional strategies in higher education. Recent data in the country of the Philippines show that the drop-out rate in the tertiary systems is 39 percent and almost four of every ten students who enroll in the State Universities and Colleges (SUCs) drop their studies before they are able to complete their degree (Bravo, 2023). This staggering statistic is not only an educational shortcoming but is also a socioeconomic problem since dropout rates are extremely high among low-income and first-generation rural students (PHUKULE, 2025; Villanueva, 2024). These demographic disparities only highlight the disproportionate impact that the dropout crisis has on vulnerable populations, continuing cycles of educational inequality and limiting pathways to economic mobility for communities that are already marginalized.
      2. Global Impact of Student Retention and UN SDG 4 Alignment
On the global level, the World Bank (2024) describes how retention of students is one of the most important drivers of economic yield. The shortage of qualified graduates is an impediment to the global talent pipeline and hence inhibiting the innovation and expansion of the industry. Also, the work towards direct student retention will support the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal No. 4 (Quality Education) focusing on ensuring inclusive and equitable quality education and learning opportunities for all (United Nations, 2023). Likewise, Tinto (2017) and Kuh et al. (2020) stressed that academic integration, social support and institutional engagement are important predictors of student persistence. In the context of Philippines, these research findings, among others, address the need for the development of systematic support and retention models that move away from the traditional reactive model systems currently in place.
      3. Financial Implications and Post-Pandemic Challenges in Philippine Higher Education
The Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM 2, 2025) says budgetary pressures and post-pandemic disruptions remain the biggest Kano of attrition in Philippine SUCs. The post-COVID-19 pandemic scenario has been defined as 'learning crisis', and enrolment trends are likely to display huge disparities from 2020 to 2025 (PHKULE, 2025). These variations are indicative of set academic and socio-economic pressures that threaten the value of SUCs as contributors towards national development and as suppliers of the labor market. The compounding financial constraints, in conjunction with pandemic-related disruptions, have demanded unprecedented challenges for the institutional retention efforts requiring innovative solutions outside the traditional resource allocation models.
      4. CHED Student Affairs Framework and Reactive Academic Support Systems
In the context of the Philippines, the Commission on Higher Education (CHED) has prescribed student support services under Memorandum Order No. 09, Series of 2013, particularly the intervention of advising, counseling, and tutoring. However, most of these systems are responsive in nature, that is, they only respond to students after showing students that they are disinterested or that they are performing poorly (CHED, 2013; Villanueva, 2025). This reactive orientation, although well-intentioned, results in time lags between when academic difficulty begins to evolve and when institutional response occurs so that problems can compound before intervention occurs. Similar difficulties in international contexts are noticed in research by Yukselturk et al. (2021) in which the absence of early monitoring systems caused high attrition rates in online courses in Turkish universities. Tempelaar and Rienties (2022) established that traditional advising methods are very subjective and inconsistent which also emphasised the need for algorithmic support systems that can assist in identifying at-risk students during the initial stages of academic difficulty.
      5. Institutional Resource Constraints and Fragmented Data Systems
As highlighted by Sayo et al. (2022), faculty and academic advisers usually have high workloads that limit early intervention in warning signs like irregular attendance, late submission of work or unusual lack of non-participation logged in Learning Management Systems (LMS). The problem is compounded in rural SUCs where low resources, poor connectivity and unequal access to technology reduces the possibility of timely interventions (Hara et al., 2023). Moreover, according to Arao et al. (2025), institutions are not able to align student records and behavioral data from various different platforms that leads to the missed opportunities for proactive intervention due to fragmented data systems. This reliance on reactive modalities leads to inflated cost of remediation and reduced academic durability, thus calling for an urgent need for advocacy of data-driven strategies, which is capable of predicting and preventing drop out before it occurs. The combination of limited human resources, fragmented technological infrastructure, and institutional resource limitations constitute a systemic environment in which it is still infeasible to identify students at-risk in their early stages through traditional support mechanisms.
      6. Emergence of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Education
Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) has changed the education industry with predictive, personalized, and adaptive learning environments. Zawacki-Richter et al. (2023) and Ahmed & Haseeb (2024) this accuracy of academic level tracking through AI-powered learning analytics may be increased by more than 85 percent to predict educational performance and involvement trends. De la Cruz (2025) states that LMS platforms such as Canvas and Moodle that contain intelligent systems make use of massive amounts of data to determine learning patterns that are invisible to the human eye. Additionally, Alotaibi & Alshehri (2025) demonstrate that predictive analytics can help universities anticipate academic performance and take quantitative action as a result by delving from traditional academic advising to proactive and data-driven decision making. On a global level, Siemens and Long (2021) offer some light on the place of learning analytics, as a middle ground between data science and pedagogy. Gaescic et al. (2022) similarly demonstrated the significant benefits of predictive analytics systems in enhancing institutional decision-making - specifically, answering the question as to the correlation between engagement, assessment patterns and subsequent retention with elucidative processes. In the Philippines, this shift is part of the Department of Education's digital transformation strategy, which involves embracing the use of data-driven education systems to improve retention and well-being (DepEd, 2024).
      7. Machine Learning Models and Philippine AI Tools for Education
AI-based early warning systems encourage predictive analytics to identify the students who are at risk and intervene in a timely manner. Baticulon et al. (2025) have shown that machine learning algorithms can predict the probabilities of dropouts weeks or even months in advance of the critical points of failures. Maguigad et al. (2025) also confirmed that the interaction of behavioural and demographic data leads to better model fit and interpretability. Cortez and Silva (2022) have found that algorithms such as Random Forest and Neural Network can achieve the prediction accuracy of 90 percent for predicting student success from data harvested using LMS. In the Philippine context, AI tools like SIGLA and TALINO have produced measurable increases in student engagement between 15 per cent and 20 per cent, through automatic feedback and adaptive learning pathways (Guevara et al., 2025). These results are in line with other sectors where the use of AI in predictive analysis has played a role in lowering the rate of error on operations by as much as 30 percent such as in the cases of healthcare and finance (Hassan et al., 2025). The case of machine learning algorithms obtained efficacy in educational prediction and successful Philippines government initiatives in the AI implementation prove that the technical feasibility of developing localized EW systems in SUC contexts is feasible.
      8. Ethical Considerations and Algorithmic Bias in Educational AI
Hwang and Chang (2023) contended that adaptive feedback mechanisms are pivotal in the process of preventing students from being academically disengaged and helping them persist. However, data-governance and ethical issues continue to be critical issues. Holmes et al. (2021) and Instructure (2025) warn of biases in AI algorithms and the subsequent risk of perpetuating inequalities if the algorithms are trained on non-universal and inadequate datasets. These risks require robust ethical guidelines that can help ensure AI instruments will foster equity and not inadvertently accomplish relationships among susceptible groups of students. The tension between maximizing the predictive power of AI while ensuring fairness in the algorithms that power educational AI systems calls for deliberate design practices that prioritize equity, transparency, and accountability in educational AI systems, especially in resource-constrained Philippine SUC contexts.
      9. Implementation Gap and Need for Localized AI Solutions
Despite these developments, there are critical research gaps found in literature. Liao et al. (2025) and Mahmood et al. (2025) share that most educational predictive analytics frameworks have been at the theoretical or pilot level and do not yet represent institutional buy-in as well as sustainable operation integration. Most existing early warning systems have been trialled in controlled research settings or in small-scale pilot settings rather than at scale in diverse institutional settings, with varied technological maturity and resource profiles. In the Philippine SUC context specifically, zero comprehensive AI-enabled ICT early warning system has been operationalised with integration to CHED's institutional support framework; as well as continued adoption beyond experimental programmes. This implementation gap - between proven research and localised, scalable implementation - is a critical gap that requires frameworks that are built specifically for the Philippine SUC operational realities, resource constraints and a variety of institutional contexts dating from well-resourced urban universities to under-resourced rural institutions.
      10. Research Gaps: Demographic Bias, Psychosocial Factors, and Rural-Urban Divides
A systematic review of 51 research conducted by Maguigad et al. (2025) identified three common shortcomings in current predictive analytics models. First, existing models give too much attention to demographic and performance data and do not account for psychosocial data such as motivation, mental health, self-efficacy, and sense of belonging - all of which are malleable through educational interventions and crucial for early warning systems that can action them. Second, existing studies do not adequately address the unique rural-urban digital disadvantage for SUCs given the evidence of unique psychosocial challenges faced by rural students and infrastructure challenges impacting access to technology enabled support systems. Third, often models are optimized in terms of prediction accuracy metrics, not necessarily actionability, producing alerts but not prescriptive guidance for educators with respect to differentiated intervention strategies based on individual student risk profiles, and institutional capacity constraints. These gaps signal the need for a local customizable adaptive AI-enabled framework combining multimodal data sources (from LMS logs to attendance to sentiment analysis) to provide actionable and equitable early warning alerts responsive to Philippine SUC contexts.
      11. Ensemble Machine Learning Models and Real-Time Dashboard Systems
Recent studies have indicated that ensemble machine learning techniques, which aggregate predictions from different machine learning methods, are more precise than using individual ones in order to select at-risk students. When these methods use data from a variety of sources, such as LMS logs, attendance, behavior, and academic performance, they can manage to have accuracy rates above 90 percent. This multi-source approach gives a more complete picture of student risk.
Real-time dashboards also play an important role by translating these predictions into practical guidance for educators. By displaying attendance trends, grades, behavioral notes and past interventions all in one spot, these dashboards are able to help teachers and advisers quickly identify which students need to be supported. Automated alerts provide added safeguards to make sure an intervention will occur before academic problems escalate to seriousness.
Overall, success of ensemble models, data integration and dashboard visualization are indicative of successful implementation of AI-powered early warning systems that are effective and feasible. These tools provide a solid basis for the development of an AEWS relevant for the Philippine SUCs and can fulfill the role of supporting the institutions in improving student retention.
      12. The Role of Counseling and Psychosocial Support in Student Retention
Research is increasing to show that psychosocial factors are pivotal determinants of student persistence in higher education. Lipson et al. (2019) reported that mental health issues, especially anxiety and depression, have a powerful effect on academic achievement and retention, with about 30% of college students reporting on the negative effects of mental health problems on their academic achievement. In the Philippine context, where unique socioeconomic pressures and cultural transitions within the school environment may be encountered by students, this may be particularly salient.
Auer and Griffiths (2023) showed that early identification of psychosocial risk factors, such as social isolation, financial stress and emotional distress, can lead to an increase of 22% in retention interventions when compared to academic-only early warning systems. Their longitudinal study of 5,000 university students found that psychosocial indicators can often present earlier than academic deterioration, and therefore, there is a huge window of opportunity to proactively intervene. This aligns with findings by Pritchard and Wilson (2023), who documented that integrated support systems addressing both academic and mental health needs achieved significantly higher retention rates (87%) compared to systems focused exclusively on academic performance (72%).
These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating counseling perspectives and psychosocial metrics into early warning systems, particularly in resource-constrained environments like Philippine SUCs where students may face compounded challenges. By expanding beyond traditional academic indicators to include holistic student wellbeing assessment, early warning systems can trigger appropriate interventions—whether academic mentoring, counseling referrals, or other support services—tailored to the root causes of student distress.








   2. Related System        
      1. International Early Warning Systems: Course Signals and eAdvisor
Early Warning Systems (EWS) have proved to be a success in the International higher education system. Course Signals at Purdue University predicts the risk of student failure based on data about course management, patterns of engagement and academic performance. 87.42% retention of exposed students and 69.40% retention of non-exposed students. Similarly, eAdvisor Phoenix, AZ at Arizona State University tracks student's progress and create focused intervention and Georgia State University's predictive analytics system process 800+ data points daily for 40k students creating 90k interventions annually and helping us grow 7 percentage points in our four year graduation rate (Brown et al., 2022; Hanover Research, 2025).
However, these systems are designed to function better under high-resource institutional settings, in which digital infrastructure is robust, data management systems are centralized, appropriate IT staff are present, enterprise-level budgets for software licenses exist, reliable connectivity is available, and student populations have access to the devices and internet on a regular basis. These prerequisites are the basic barriers to implementation amongst Philippine SUCs where there is fragmentation in their infrastructure, lack of IT capacity, scarcity of resources, and unequal digital access to misunderstand the situations when international EWS were designed (UNSEO, 2023).




      2. Philippine Context: SIGLA and TALINO
The Department of Education has piloted AI programs such as SIGLA (health monitoring and adaptive testing) and TALINO (geospatial resource mapping), both of which have shown a 15-20% engagement gain through adaptive feedback. However, such systems are placed inside discrete subdistance student aptitude prediction subdomain applications rather than whole college-level student aggravation prediction systems. Their applicability in different SUC institutional settings and their integration into holistic retention systems are still limited, suggesting that existing Philippine AI efforts do not have institutional-scale early warning system implementation (Guevara et al., 2025).
      3. Implementation Gap
A gap formed between international EWS precedents and Philippine institutional realities indicates a critical implementation chasm. Successful e-learning for the poor or in middle-income countries, not limited to adapted software solutions, will need redesigned systems to take into account infrastructure constraints, institutional capacity limitations and unique psychosocial risk profiles of poor student populations.
      4. Proposed Framework
This research proposes an AI enabled Predictive Analytics framework explicitly designed in Philippine SUC contexts this time tilling the boundaries of behavioral, demographic, and psychosocial markers that generate actionable early warning signals. Unlike international systems, compatibility with fragmented existing data infrastructure, feasibility for resource constrained staff without extensive IT staff, culturally appropriate intervention recommendation which reflects institutional capacity, the explicit attention to the digital divide between rural and urban communities, and the seamless integration with CHED's Student Affairs and Services mandate were given high priority. This local-based strategy is a translation of theoretical EWS efficacy to an institutional deployment aimed for sustainable improvements in retention in Philippine SUC contexts such as discrete measurable retention improvements.
   3. Concept of the Study
  















Figure 2-1. Conceptual Diagram of Academic Early Warning System (AEWS)
The Academic Early Warning System (AEWS) is designed as a machine-learning-assisted and role-based web platform that supports the early identification of students who may require academic, personal, or psychosocial support. The system processes structured academic and support-related information, including grades, attendance records, prior GPA, failed subjects, academic performance indicators, referral information, and needs-assessment responses.
Rather than presenting only a single predictive risk score, the system provides explanatory outputs such as risk levels, risk scores, contributing indicators, academic performance weaknesses, attendance concerns, and other relevant risk drivers. These outputs are intended to support informed review and referral decisions; they do not represent confirmed causes or clinical diagnoses. Human decision-making remains central to the planning and delivery of student support.
The system implements role-based workflows for instructors, administrators, AMU staff, and students. Instructors can manage classes, upload classlists, gradesheets, and attendance records, review student risk indicators, generate class-level predictions, and submit academic or support-related referrals. Administrators can manage user accounts, approve or reject account requests, monitor system activity, configure needs-assessment forms, and generate institutional reports.
AMU staff can review assigned referrals, examine student academic and assessment information, send needs-assessment invitations, view submitted responses, generate or review support-related predictions, document support-routing decisions, and produce referral and assessment reports. Students can access their authenticated dashboard, view relevant academic information, receive notifications, and complete assigned needs assessments through the system.
The AEWS supports both machine-learning-based prediction and rule-based or heuristic assessment, depending on the workflow and the availability of student data. Its primary purpose is to assist with early risk identification, referral triage, needs-assessment data gathering, support routing, documentation, and limited follow-up tracking. The system supplements existing university support procedures by providing a centralized, organized, and scalable platform while preserving the authority of instructors, administrators, and AMU staff in making final intervention and support decisions.

   6. Methods
This chapter describes the systematic strategy to be used in the design, development and evaluation of the AI-based Academic Early Warning System (AEWS) for Bukidnon State University. It gives details on the materials, software, and hardware configuration that will be used, followed by the research design and development process model for project execution. It goes on to explain the process of data collection, preprocessing, machine learning model development, system integration, as well as detailed evaluation strategies. The methods are designed to meet the specific goals of the study to ensure that there is a robust and user-centered system that will ensure accurate risk prediction and early academic interventions. Through an iterative process of agile development and rigorous testing this chapter provides a clear framework for developing a scalable, reliable, and effective AEWS solution.
      8. Research Design
This study will follow a Design Science Research (DSR) paradigm that is widely used in information systems literature in order to build and evaluate innovative artifacts for solving identified organizational problems. The process will follow the established DSR process model by Peffers et al, which consists of six activities which are sequentially but iterative: Problem Identification and Motivation, Definition of Objectives for a Solution, Design and Development, Demonstration, Evaluation and Communication. Our main artifact is the integrated platform of AEWS software, and the utility of our software will be assessed in its authentic organizational context, the student support framework of Bukidnon State University.
The method of the research would be of mixed type, with a combination of both quantitative and qualitative methods at various stages. A quasi-experimental component will be incorporated in the evaluation phase to compare retention and performance measures of a cohort of students exposed to the AEWS-driven interventions as compared with a similar historical control group from past academic years. Simultaneously, a qualitative case study component will include in-depth, semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions with faculty advisors and AMU staff post-implementation to probe themes of usability, integrating workflow, and perceived effectiveness. This approach is consistent with the philosophy of pragmatism which emphasizes practical solutions and values both objective measures and subjective experiential knowledge in order to fully understand the impact of the artifact.
Ethical considerations will be brought to the front throughout the design. Beyond complying with data privacy laws we will conduct what is called algorithmic impact assessments to proactively review the potential risks of bias or unfair exclusion in the data predictive model. We will also put in place a human-in-the-loop protocol, by which all AI-generated risk flags will be reviewed by a designated staff member to ensure that any type of formal intervention is not triggered unless a human safety net is in place, thereby keeping humans in the loop and accountable for the consequential decisions made that impact students.

Requirements analysis focused on the academic monitoring and AMU referral workflow currently supported by the institution. Instead of depending on direct live integration with LMS or SIS platforms, the implemented system supports data entry and file-based uploading of classlists, gradesheets, attendance records, and needs-assessment data. This approach was selected to match the available institutional infrastructure and to allow instructors and AMU staff to operate the system even without centralized API access to external university systems. The requirements were therefore centered on practical system functions such as secure account access, class and student management, academic record processing, risk prediction, referral handling, needs-assessment management, notifications, reporting, and activity logging.
The data includes student’s attendance records, gradesheet records, and needs-assessment responses, which were integrated using the common Student_ID field. Student_ID ensures that each student record could be connected across academic performance, attendance behavior, and support-related indicators. 
Students’ attendance records were used to determine each student’s attendance rate. Attendance is an important indicator of academic engagement because frequent absences may limit students’ participation in lectures, laboratory activities, assessments, and other course requirements. For the system, attendance data were converted into a numerical attendance-rate feature ranging from 0 to 100 percent. This feature enabled the predictive model to identify patterns between attendance behavior and students’ likelihood of being classified as Low Risk or High Risk.
Gradesheet records were used to represent students’ current and previous academic performance. The extracted academic indicators included previous Grade Point Average (GPA), failed-subject count, midterm grade, class standing, laboratory grade, major output grade, and final grade. Previous GPA and failed-subject count reflected prior academic performance and possible academic difficulties, while midterm grades and other grading components reflected a student’s current performance in the course. The final grade was also used to create the risk label required for supervised machine learning model training.
Needs-assessment responses were used to identify academic and external factors that may affect student performance. Individual responses were analyzed and summarized into two grouped indicators: academic challenge score and external factor score. The academic challenge score represented learning-related concerns, such as difficulty understanding lectures, weak study habits, low motivation, and difficulty with specific subjects. Meanwhile, the external factor score represented non-academic concerns, including financial difficulties, health-related concerns, family issues, part-time work, and mental health-related concerns. These indicators allowed the system to consider circumstances beyond grades and attendance when assessing student risk.
Before model development, the consolidated dataset underwent data profiling to examine its structure, completeness, validity, and consistency. The data requirements included attendance rate, midterm grade, final grade, previous GPA, failed-subject count, and needs-assessment indicators. Attendance values were verified to fall within the valid range of 0 to 100 percent, while grade-related fields were checked for consistency with the BukSU grading scale of 1.00 to 5.00. The datasets were also checked for missing values, duplicate records, and inconsistencies after integration through Student_ID.
The profiling results showed that the prepared training dataset contained 1,000 student records. After validation and preprocessing, no missing values remained in the model-ready dataset. The class distribution consisted of 410 Low Risk records and 590 High Risk records. These results indicate that the dataset was complete, valid, and suitable for feature extraction and student-risk prediction.
After profiling and validation, the raw institutional records were transformed into numerical features suitable for the XGBoost classification model. The engineered features included previous GPA, failed-subject count, attendance rate, academic challenge score, external factor score, midterm grade, class standing, laboratory grade, and major output grade. These features were selected because they represented meaningful academic, behavioral, and personal indicators related to student performance and potential academic risk.
The final grade was used to generate the supervised risk label for model training. Students with final grades from 1.00 to 2.25 were labeled as Low Risk, while students with final grades from 2.50 to 5.00 were labeled as High Risk. This classification followed the BukSU grading scale, in which higher numerical grades indicate weaker academic performance.

The AEWS thus shall become an integrated web-based decision-support/referral management platform, blending role-based system support, predictive risk categorization, SHAP based interpretability and AMU workflow support within a single operating context. 	   14. Machine Learning Model Optimization with XGBoost
The predictive analytics component of the AEWS utilizes Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), an ensemble machine learning algorithm suitable for structured tabular datasets. The model is designed to classify students into binary risk categories: Low Risk (Label 0) for students with final grades from 1.00 to 2.25, and High Risk (Label 1) for students with final grades from 2.50 to 5.00. This labeling rule follows the BukSU grading scale, where higher numerical grades indicate weaker academic performance.
The deployed model uses nine core predictive features available through the implemented AEWS workflow. These features are previous GPA, failed subject count, attendance rate, academic challenge score, external factor score, midterm grade, class standing, laboratory grade, and major output grade. The features are extracted from uploaded gradesheets, attendance records, and needs-assessment responses, allowing the system to generate predictions without relying on live LMS or SIS integration.
Before prediction, uploaded records undergo cleaning, normalization, matching, and validation. Student identifiers are standardized, numeric fields are converted into model-ready values, attendance rates are normalized into a consistent percentage format, and grade values are interpreted according to the BukSU 1.00 to 5.00 grading scale. Needs-assessment responses are converted into academic challenge and external factor scores based on selected student responses. These preprocessing steps ensure that the input supplied to the XGBoost model is consistent with the feature structure used during model development.
The model was evaluated using standard classification metrics generated from the saved model evaluation results. The selected student-risk model achieved 97.5% holdout accuracy, with weighted precision, recall, and F1-score values of approximately 0.975. Cross-validation results were also recorded to support the reliability of the model evaluation. These results indicate that the model performed consistently in classifying student risk using the available structured academic and needs-assessment data.
Model interpretability was supported through SHAP-based analysis during model evaluation. SHAP was used to examine the contribution of each input variable to the trained XGBoost model and to identify which features had the strongest influence on risk classification. In the deployed system, prediction explanations are presented through interpretable contributing indicators such as previous GPA, failed subject count, attendance rate, midterm grade, class standing, laboratory grade, major output grade, academic challenge score, and external factor score. These explanations help users understand the basis of the prediction while preserving human judgment in final referral and support decisions.
The predictive component is integrated into the AEWS as a decision-support tool rather than as an automatic decision-maker. Prediction outputs are interpreted together with institutional referral criteria, uploaded academic records, needs-assessment responses, and AMU review. This design allows the system to support early identification and referral prioritization while ensuring that instructors, administrators, and AMU staff remain responsible for reviewing student cases and determining appropriate support actions.

The XGBoost student academic-risk prediction model was evaluated using a confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1-score, and Receiver Operating Characteristic–Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC). These measures provided complementary perspectives on the model’s classification performance. The confusion matrix identified the number and type of correct and incorrect predictions, precision assessed the correctness of High Risk alerts, recall measured the detection of actual High Risk students, F1-score summarized the balance between precision and recall, and ROC-AUC assessed the model’s ability to distinguish between the two risk categories using predicted probability scores. Using these measures together allowed the evaluation to consider both overall predictive performance and the practical consequences of classification errors in the Academic Early Warning System (AEWS).
The evaluation used an 80:20 stratified train-test split of the 1,000 prepared student records. Of these records, 800 were used to train the model, while 200 were reserved as the holdout test dataset. The full dataset contained 410 Low Risk students and 590 High Risk students. Stratification preserved these proportions in the holdout dataset, which contained 82 Low Risk students and 118 High Risk students. Maintaining the class proportions allowed the evaluation to reflect the distribution of the prepared dataset and ensured that both categories were represented during testing.
The actual risk labels were derived from students’ final grades. Students with final grades from 1.00 to 2.25 were classified as Low Risk, while students with final grades from 2.50 to 5.00 were classified as High Risk. Accordingly, the evaluation measured how closely the model’s predictions matched these final-grade-based categories. Final grades served as the basis for establishing the actual outcomes rather than as input features for the selected model. The prediction features included previous GPA, failed-subject count, attendance rate, academic challenge score, external factor score, midterm grade, class standing, laboratory grade, and major output grade.
High Risk, represented by Label 1, was treated as the positive class, while Low Risk, represented by Label 0, was treated as the negative class. This designation reflected the system’s primary purpose: identifying students who may require academic monitoring, referral, or intervention. The terms “positive” and “negative” therefore refer to the statistical classification categories rather than to favorable or unfavorable academic performance.

      18. Confusion Matrix Components (TP, FP, FN, TN)
The confusion matrix was used to examine the relationship between the actual risk categories and the categories predicted by the model. It provided the foundation for calculating precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy. More importantly, it showed the specific types of errors that could affect the system’s academic monitoring and intervention processes.
1. True Positives (TP): The number of students who are actually High Risk and are correctly predicted as High Risk by the model. Example: A student with a final grade of 3.00 (High Risk) is flagged as High Risk by the AEWS.
2. False Positives (FP): The number of students who are actually Low Risk but are incorrectly predicted as High Risk by the model (Type I error). Example: A student performing well with a 1.75 final grade (Low Risk) is incorrectly flagged as High Risk.
3. False Negatives (FN): The number of students who are actually High Risk but are incorrectly predicted as Low Risk by the model (Type II error). Example: A struggling student with a 3.00 final grade (High Risk) is missed by the system and classified as Low Risk.
4. True Negatives (TN): The number of students who are actually Low Risk and are correctly predicted as Low Risk by the model. Example: A student with a 1.50 final grade (Low Risk) is correctly classified as Low Risk by the system.

\[ Accuracy = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \]
\[ Accuracy = \frac{115 + 80}{115 + 80 + 2 + 3} = \frac{195}{200} = 0.975\ (97.5\%) \]

\[ Error\ Rate = \frac{FP + FN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \]
\[ Error\ Rate = \frac{2 + 3}{200} = \frac{5}{200} = 0.025\ (2.5\%) \]

Out of 118 actual High Risk students, 115 were correctly identified (TP), while 3 were incorrectly classified as Low Risk (FN). Out of 82 actual Low Risk students, 80 were correctly identified (TN), while 2 were incorrectly classified as High Risk (FP). These results demonstrate a high degree of classification precision and consistency in identifying student risk within the holdout evaluation set.
      19. F1-Score
The F1-score was used to evaluate the balance between precision and recall, serving as their harmonic mean. This metric is especially important in an early warning system because prioritizing precision alone could lead to missing at-risk students (false negatives), while focusing solely on recall could result in unnecessary interventions for low-risk students (false positives). The F1-score is calculated as:
\[ F1 = 2 \times \frac{Precision \times Recall}{Precision + Recall} \]
Using the holdout test set values for High Risk students (Precision = 0.983, Recall = 0.975), the F1-score is calculated as:
\[ F1 = 2 \times \frac{0.983 \times 0.975}{0.983 + 0.975} = \frac{1.91685}{1.958} \approx 0.979\ (97.9\%) \]
An F1-score of 0.979 indicates that the model maintains a strong balance between correctly identifying High Risk students and minimizing false alarms. From an institutional perspective, this ensures that support resources and AMU interventions are directed efficiently to students who truly need assistance without overburdening academic advisors or creating unnecessary distress for low-risk students.
      20. Precision
Precision was used to determine the correctness of the model’s High Risk predictions. It measures the proportion of students predicted as High Risk who were actually High Risk according to the final-grade-based labels:
\[ Precision = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \]
The model predicted 119 students as High Risk. Of these students, 115 were actual High Risk cases and four were actual Low Risk cases. Therefore:
\[ Precision = \frac{115}{115 + 4} = \frac{115}{119} \approx 0.9664 = 96.64\% \]
The precision of 96.64% means that approximately 97 out of every 100 High Risk predictions were correct within the evaluated dataset. The remaining 3.36% of High Risk predictions were incorrect alerts:
\[ Incorrect\ Alert\ Proportion = \frac{FP}{TP + FP} = \frac{4}{119} \approx 3.36\% \]
This result indicates that the model’s alerts were largely consistent with the actual risk labels. Such performance is useful in supporting academic monitoring because faculty and AMU personnel may have limited time and resources for reviewing flagged students. A high proportion of correct alerts can help make the review process more focused and manageable.
However, precision does not indicate whether the system detected all students who were actually High Risk. A model could achieve high precision by flagging only a small number of very clear cases while missing other students who require attention. Consequently, the obtained precision must be interpreted alongside recall.
Precision also depends on the composition of the evaluated population. High Risk students represented 59.00% of the holdout dataset. If the system is applied to a population with a different proportion of High Risk students, the precision may change even if other aspects of model performance remain similar. The reported 96.64% should therefore be interpreted as an observed result for this dataset rather than a guaranteed alert accuracy for every future cohort.
      21. Recall
Recall was used to evaluate the ability of the model to identify students who were truly High Risk. It measures the proportion of actual High Risk students that were correctly detected by the system. Recall is calculated as:
\[ Recall = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \]
A high recall score indicates that the model was able to identify most students who genuinely required academic attention or intervention. This is particularly important in an academic early warning system because failing to identify a struggling student may prevent timely support from being provided.
For example, if there were 50 actual High Risk students and the model correctly identified 40 of them, the recall score would be:
\[ Recall = \frac{40}{50} = 0.80 \]
This means that the model detected 80% of all actual High Risk students, while 20% were not identified. These missed students are considered false negatives.
Recall was included in the evaluation because the main objective of the system is to identify students who may need academic support before their academic standing further declines. A low recall score would indicate that the system is failing to detect a considerable number of at-risk students.
      22. ROC-AUC
The Receiver Operating Characteristic–Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC) was considered as an additional evaluation metric for measuring the model’s ability to distinguish between Low Risk and High Risk students. Unlike precision, recall, and F1-score, which are based on final predicted labels, ROC-AUC evaluates the probability scores generated by the model.
The model assigns each student a probability of belonging to the High Risk class. A threshold, commonly 0.50, may be used to convert this probability into a final classification. For example, a student with a predicted High Risk probability of 0.75 may be classified as High Risk, while a student with a probability of 0.20 may be classified as Low Risk.
The ROC curve is formed by plotting the True Positive Rate against the False Positive Rate at different classification thresholds. The True Positive Rate is calculated as:
\[ True\ Positive\ Rate = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \]
The False Positive Rate is calculated as:
\[ False\ Positive\ Rate = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} \]
The area under this curve is the ROC-AUC score. Its value ranges from 0 to 1. A value of 1.00 indicates perfect discrimination between High Risk and Low Risk students, while a value of 0.50 indicates performance comparable to random classification. A higher ROC-AUC value indicates that the model is more capable of assigning higher risk probabilities to actual High Risk students and lower probabilities to actual Low Risk students.
Although the current implementation stores holdout accuracy, cross-validation accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, ROC-AUC remains applicable because the model generates probability outputs. The ROC-AUC may therefore be included in future model evaluations to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model’s ability to distinguish between students who are likely to require intervention and those who are not.
